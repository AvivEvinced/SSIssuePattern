Root-cause tree vs GT symptom classes

The two views answer different questions. The GT class records what the customer saw (a failed scan, 0 pages, failed pages, odd page counts, anything else). The root-cause tree records why it happened and who fixes it. Each ticket has one of each, so they can be cross-tabulated.

Cell = number of tickets. Shading = share of the GT class (row). Normalized mutual information between the views: (0 = unrelated, 1 = the same partition).

Where they look alike

  • GT 1 “scan/crawl failure” ≈ family E (platform). 17 of 28 class-1 tickets (61%) are Evinced-side: outages, scheduler bugs, dispatch failures, limits. This is the strongest one-to-one link.
  • GT 2 “0 pages” ≈ B + C. 18 of 25 (72%) are login (12) or crawl-scope (6) problems. Zero pages almost always means the crawler never got past the first page.
  • GT 3 “failed pages” ≈ A + D. 10 of 14 (71%) are blocked pages or heavy/crashing pages.
  • GT 4 “crawl irregularities” ≈ A + C. 22 of 29 (76%) are blocking/rate-limiting or scope configuration.
  • Families F, G, H ≈ subdivisions of GT 5. 98%, 100% and 86% of them are class 5. For non-incident tickets the root-cause tree is simply a finer split of “Other”.

Where they differ

  • The same cause produces different symptoms. Families A, B and H each appear in all five GT classes; 21 of the 42 sub-issues span more than one class. A WAF block (A1) shows up as “0 pages”, “failed pages”, “page count low” or “Other” depending on how much of the site it blocked.
  • “Other” hides incidents. 53 of the 183 class-5 tickets (29%) are real crawl/scan/platform incidents (families A–E): logins that broke, blocks raised as configuration questions, outages reported as “Error loading”.
  • The GT classes are not exclusive at the edges. Readers flagged tickets whose GT class looks wrong or mixed (e.g. 1 page crawled filed as “0 pages”, a WFA bug filed under Site Scanner).
  • Different use. GT classes are good for triage dashboards (what is the customer experiencing). They cannot drive a fix: “0 pages” spans 11 different sub-issues with different fixes. The root-cause tree is the right key for the investigator’s recommendation; the GT class is a useful prior.
Practical reading: use the GT class as the entry point (symptom → shortlist of sub-issues, see the “By GT symptom class” tab of the taxonomy page), then use logs to pick the sub-issue. That is exactly the role of the log tree below.

Can the root-cause tree be called data-driven?

Partly. The labels are grounded in data; the structure is expert judgment checked against data. The honest description is evidence-based, expert-structured, partially validated. Scorecard:

What would make it fully data-driven

  1. Have a support engineer adjudicate the disagreements of the blind re-label (listed below) and adjust definitions where both readings are defensible (A1 vs A4, B4 vs B1, E1 vs E5).
  2. Label the next 50–100 tickets prospectively with the tree (support picks the sub-issue at close). Agreement and the share landing in H5 tell you whether the tree holds up.
  3. Use the log tree as an independent signal: for tickets inside log retention, the log leaf that fires should agree with the sub-issue. The back-test is the first, small version of that.
  4. Merge thin sub-issues (A6, A8, A9, C7 have one ticket each) or keep them only where they have a distinct fix and a distinct log signal.
Blind re-label: all pairs

Log tree: how issues appear in the logs

Three layers, written so a support engineer can walk it without knowing the code: 1 · Stage — where in the run it went wrong; 2 · Symptom — what the logs say, in plain words; 3 · Pattern — the concrete log pattern, the root-cause sub-issues it points to, and what to check next. Signature ids link to the catalog with the ready-to-run DataPrime query.

Four rules before you search: (1) Anchor on the time of the failing run, not the ticket date. (2) HTTP status lines are logged at INFO — never filter by ERROR when looking for blocks. (3) Match message text, not template ids (they change every release); in DataPrime ~ 'a|b' is a literal substring — use separate ~ terms with ||. (4) Judge by share within one job or host (e.g. 403 on most URLs), not by global counts: a few big sites dominate every error volume.

Suggested taxonomy changes from the log research

  • Add a sub-issue for worker-side engine package failures (ENGINES_PLUGIN_ERROR with ENOENT/ENOTEMPTY/zlib, ~1,000/week): Evinced-side, currently mixed into D2 “engine crash”.
  • Add the content-type case bug (“text/HTML; charset=utf-8 is not allowed”) as a product bug under C.
  • Rule for B2: when a login selector fails, check the same job for 403/405 first — in #3301 the “missing selector” was really a blocked login page (A1).

Next steps

  1. Turn the high-discrimination patterns (S1.1a, S1.2a, S1.3a, S2.1a, S3.1a/b, S3.2, S4.1, S4.2, S5.1a, S6.1a, S7.2) into saved Coralogix views or alerts keyed by property.
  2. Give the investigator a per-property “run report” query: status-code histogram, final failure types, visited-page max, interaction errors and scope rejections for one job — most leaves are decided from those five numbers.
  3. Extend the back-test to 40+ tickets once the run-time anchoring is scripted (most misses were runs outside the searched window).

Method and caveats

  • Tickets: 279 tickets from the “Site Scanner Ticket Classification” sheet; full Zendesk threads including internal notes. Labelled by 9 parallel readers into free-text labels, then consolidated by hand into 42 sub-issues / 8 families (scripts/taxonomy.py).
  • Reproducibility: a separate reader labelled 70 random tickets using only the definitions (no access to the first labels). Both readers are AI models, so agreement measures consistency of the definitions, not correctness.
  • Logs: Coralogix (us2), applications site-scanner, admin, front. Four research passes (page-level, access & login, lifecycle & platform, back-test), ~150 queries, windows mostly 2026-10-27 → 11-03; back-test windows May–Oct 2026. Logs before ~June 2026 have a different format and are too thin for back-testing. A fifth pass re-ran every signature query.
  • Counts reflect ticket volume (what customers reported), not incident frequency in production.
  • Files: data/ticket_labels.csv, data/taxonomy.json, data/analysis/agreement.json, data/logs/*.md|json, data/logtree.json, scripts/logtree.py. Raw Zendesk threads are not committed (they contain credentials).
